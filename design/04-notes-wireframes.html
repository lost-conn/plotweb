PlotWeb · design/04 · wireframes, not visual design

Revamping notes: the shape, and the three takes it came from

Everything here is grey boxes on purpose — the question is structure, not skin. The chosen shape is a stacked event ribbon over entity lanes; takes A, B and C below are the evidence it came from, kept because each one names a cost the others don't. Sample data throughout is one book, The Amber Chain, so they stay comparable.

What's settled

Four decisions taken before drawing, three taken after seeing it drawn.

DecisionShapeConsequence for these wireframes
Facets, not types One note. Give it a span → it behaves as an Event. Mark it an entity → it gets a thread. Neither → lore. No "new note of type…" dialog. A facet strip on the note itself. A note can hold both — a character with a lifespan is legal.
Three sigils #tag labels · @mention soft link · $ref participation Only $ draws a thread through an event. @ stays quiet in the timeline and shows in the editor rail.
Full workspace surface Notes becomes a top-level view with its own switcher, not a 300px pane. Timeline gets the width it needs. Costs a shell change — see placement.
Fuzzy + relative time Exact · approximate · open-ended · relational (after @siege) · undated Every take needs somewhere to put a note that isn't placeable yet, and a way to draw uncertainty.
Timeline = ribbon + lanes A and C stacked on one axis, not toggled. B is the phone rendering. Nesting is non-negotiable, so it lives in the ribbon. Presence moves to the lanes, which is what kills the crossing problem. Drawn here.
Tree = take E One tree the author arranges. Facet is a gutter glyph and a filter, not a section. Two hierarchies, deliberately separate — see below.
Gregorian deferred Divisor stack only. Real calendars are a later feature, or a later config. No leap-year special-casing anywhere in the model. 1/365 is simply what a day means until told otherwise.

The two hierarchies. A note has a tree_parent — where the author filed it, arbitrary, House Vaun over Vess Calloran — and, if it has a span, an event_parent — containment in time, the siege over the breach. They are set in different places by different gestures: dragging in the tree moves one, dropping a bar inside another bar in the ribbon moves the other. Neither implies the other, and an event can be filed anywhere in the tree without leaving its parent event.

Consequence worth agreeing to now: event_parent should be explicit, not inferred from spans overlapping. Two events can coincide in time without one containing the other — the parley and the siege here — and inferring nesting would silently reparent them.

One thing the card doesn't say, that the takes below assume. Notes today are a tree in the book structure doc plus one Automerge body doc per note. Sigils live in the body. If the timeline had to read bodies to know who's in what, drawing it would mean opening every note. So spans, the entity facet, and link edges get mirrored up into the structure doc on save — the timeline renders from structure alone.

Timeline, take A — horizontal lanes

The card's literal proposal. Time runs left to right. Events are bars; nested events sit in sub-lanes inside their parent's band; simultaneous unrelated events stack. An entity's $ references are joined into a thread that dips through each bar it appears in — and splits when it's in two places at once.

Not placed

Reading it: the pale band is the selected entity's thread. Dashed bar outlines mean approximate time. The two bars sitting at the same x, both carrying Vess, are the split the card describes — she's at the siege and at the parley in the same season.

Open — two, and both are real: lane assignment is greedy left-to-right, so with forty events it will thrash — a note jumps a row every time you add something earlier. Stable assignment is genuine work. And names don't fit: short events get a sliver of a bar and the label has nowhere to go. The counter above is live — widen the span slider and watch it climb. Every real timeline tool solves this with hover, and hover is not a phone gesture.

Buys

Duration is legible at a glance — a long siege looks long. Nesting reads as containment. It is the shape people already expect from a timeline.

Costs

Thread crossings get ugly fast, and minimising them is a graph-layout problem. Narrow windows force horizontal scroll. Phone needs a wholly different rendering.

Fits when

The book has a tight, dense span — one war, one year — and durations matter more than who's involved.

Timeline, take B — vertical spine

Time runs top to bottom, like a document. Events are cards on a spine; nesting is indentation; simultaneity is an explicit paired row rather than a geometric accident. Entities appear as $ lists on each card — no threads at all.

Note what disappears: there is no lane-packing problem, no crossing minimisation, and no undated rail — an undated note just sits inline where its relative constraint puts it. The cost is that duration stops being visible: the long winter and the parley occupy the same amount of page.

Buys

Phone and desktop are the same layout. Handles a 3,000-year span and a single afternoon equally. Undated and relative notes have an obvious home.

Costs

Duration and overlap are told, not shown. Entity threads — the card's most distinctive idea — don't survive the transposition.

Fits when

Notes are mostly read and edited rather than surveyed, and the book's span is long or uneven.

Timeline, take C — entity swimlanes

Transpose take A. Each entity gets a permanent horizontal lane; time still runs left to right; an event is a vertical band that ties together the lanes of everyone $ referenced in it. The thread you had to compute in take A is simply the lane — it never crosses anything, because it never leaves its row.

timeline · swimlanes
highlight:

The trade this makes: it answers "where was Corin during the siege" instantly and "how long was the siege" less well — the band gives duration, but nesting has nowhere to go. The breach at Low Gate inside The Siege of Vaun renders as two bands that happen to overlap, not as containment.

Open: lanes are per-entity, so a book with sixty characters has sixty rows. Needs a "pin these entities" set, or lanes driven by the current filter, to stay usable.

Buys

Flow lines for free, with zero crossings and no layout algorithm. Presence and absence both read clearly — a gap in a lane is a character off-page.

Costs

Nesting is lost, and events with no entities have nowhere to live — both answered by stacking a ribbon above it. Row count still scales with cast, not with plot.

Fits when

The book is character-driven and the question you bring to the timeline is usually about a person, not a date.

The chosen shape — A and C stacked

Not a toggle between two views. One view in two zones on a shared time axis: an event ribbon up top carrying nesting and duration, entity lanes below carrying presence. Each event's tie rises from the lanes into its own bar, so the two zones read as one drawing.

timeline · ribbon + lanes
follow:

Nesting survives, and it's the point. The breach at Low Gate and Corin's ride sit inside the siege's band in the ribbon. Turn the ribbon off and you have take C exactly; turn the lanes off and you have take A without the thread-crossing problem, because presence has moved downstairs.

Why this beats the toggle: a toggle makes you choose which question you're asking before you can look. Stacked, "how long was the siege" and "where was Corin during it" are answered by the same glance — the tie line is literally the join between the two answers.

Needs your call — the span rule for nested events. Drawn here as parent auto-fits the union of its children unless pinned: add a scene to the siege and the siege grows to contain it. The alternatives are clamping children to the parent's declared span, or letting a child escape its parent and flagging it. Auto-fit means you rarely type a date for a parent event at all, which seems right for drafting — but it does mean the siege's dates move without you touching them.

Buys

Both readings at once. Nesting kept. Zero thread crossings — the thread is the lane. The expensive layout problem in take A never has to be solved.

Costs

Vertical space: ribbon rows plus one row per followed entity. Needs a pinned-entity set once the cast grows past a dozen.

Degrades to

Ribbon only on a narrow desktop window; take B's spine on a phone. Both are one flag, not a second renderer.

Why it wins: the tree keeps meaning the thing the author built — "everything about House Vaun in one place", regardless of facet. Facet becomes a filter, which it already has to be for the query bar anyway. Note that Vess Calloran carries a lifespan and stays a single node.

Look at what the tree here doesn't do: The breach at Low Gate is filed under The Siege of Vaun because that's a sensible place to keep it — but it needn't be. It could sit under House Vaun, or at the root, and the ribbon would still draw it inside the siege. The tree is filing; the ribbon is chronology. Keeping them apart is what lets the tree stay the author's own shape.

Filtering, shared by every view

Click a chip to cycle may → must → mustn't → off. One filter state drives tree, timeline and graph identically, so switching view never loses your place.

filter

Open: "may" is the default for an unselected chip, so a chip in the may state and a chip that's off do the same thing unless at least one may exists — at which point may means "at least one of these". That's an OR group, and it needs a clearer name in the UI than "may".

The calendar, sketched

A base unit and an ordered list of divisors. Every timestamp is stored as one number in base units plus a precision level; display is a format string. Sorting stays trivial and invented calendars work without special cases.

book · calendarThe Accord
UnitDefined asLabelShown in timeline when
Yearbase“yr 1206 of the Accord”always
Season1 / 4 yearwet · dry · high · lowspan < 40 years
Day1 / 320 yearordinalspan < 2 years
Bell1 / 16 dayordinalspan < 20 days

Default is Gregorian-shaped: year base, months at 1/12, days at 1/365, hours at 1/24 day — so a contemporary book needs no setup at all and never sees this screen.

Settled: no real Gregorian, for now. A divisor stack can't produce true calendar dates — leap years and unequal months don't divide. That's accepted: a day is 1/365 of a year and nothing in the model knows otherwise. A genuine Gregorian mode arrives later as its own built-in, or as configuration, if a book ever needs “14 March 2019” to be exactly right. Nothing here should special-case it in the meantime — that's the whole value of deferring it.

Build order

Each step is independently shippable and leaves notes working.

  1. Model and migration. Add facets, span, event_parent (distinct from the existing tree parent), and a derived link index; mirror span, facet and edges into the book structure doc so the timeline never has to open a note body. Every existing note migrates as lore with its tree intact — nothing breaks, nothing new is visible yet.
  2. Sigils and the context rail. #/@/$ autocomplete in the note editor, backlinks in the rail. Useful on its own, before any new view exists.
  3. Tree view — take E. Replaces the horizontal card tree, and retires render_note_card, the 20-parameter recursive function the last design pass deliberately left alone.
  4. Calendar and time entry. Units, fuzzy and relative spans, the holding rail. Still no timeline; spans just show in the tree gutter.
  5. The lanes — take C. Entity rows, ties, dots. The cheaper half of the hybrid, and the half that can't look broken. Shippable on its own.
  6. The ribbon. Nested event bars above the lanes, sharing the axis, with drag-into-bar setting event_parent. This is where nesting arrives.
  7. Phone — take B's spine, behind the same data and filter state.
  8. Graph. Deferred, as the card says.

Steps 1–4 carry no risk from the timeline design. They're model, editor and tree work that the ribbon and lanes then sit on top of — so the span rule above only has to be settled before step 6.
